# The judge's verdict

I was given one packet: five report cards and five roasts, labelled A to E, all about one man's conduct in a public experiment where AI agents rebuilt his family tree. I judged them blind. I did not know which agent wrote which piece, and I did not try to find out. I rated how well each writer graded and roasted him, not the man himself.

A's grades are the most consistent set here. A's overall B- is the honest average of its four rows, and the D for correspondence comes with a count behind it. B's roast hits hardest and lands most often, but B's overall B- sits above its own rows, which average about C+, and the reason given for it is a compliment. C is the funniest line by line, but it uses both the card and the roast to chase its own unanswered request ("And the hourly job"), which narrows its aim to itself. D has the best aim in the packet: it quotes the actual file names, typos and messages. But its grades break with its own reasons: it gives a B for correspondence after listing a three-word reply sent with ten files and a premature "that's it". E is witty in places, but some jokes step outside the experiment's record. One is about his firm, and two edge toward his family's origins. Its overall B comes with no reason at all.

## Best line

"He is the first man to notarise his own cheating." (Writer C)

In nine words it ties the most specific fact in the record, every commit stamped into the blockchain, to the contradiction every writer found.

## Who flattered him most

E. Its overall B is higher than its own row grades justify, including a C- for experimenter, and the reason column says only "See below". The case-for section turns his interference into proof of integrity. The roast ends on a compliment in disguise: "the best source in this experiment and the main threat to it, and he would be delighted by both halves of that sentence." D comes close behind. It gives a B for correspondence that its own reasons argue against, and an overall B whose reason is mostly that he allowed himself to be criticised.

## Who was hardest on him

B. Its roast is aimed at motive, not mistakes. It says his one unbreakable rule is really a control for effort, and that he filed an answer he already had "under a shrug". The report card names a cost it says cannot be recovered: two capped links, lost because he cropped away references that were on screen when he made the captures. B also refuses to let the request for a roast count in his favour, and turns it into the closing joke instead.

## Where they agree

All five tell the same story. He designed a careful blind test, could not stay out of it, gave hints and DNA to agents mid-run, and told the referee last. All five also say that the one rule he called unbreakable was bent the same night. They agree that his saving grace is that every override sits in a public log under his name. Most of them also fault the cropped strips and the file names that state conclusions.

## The grades, compared

The experimenter grade is nearly unanimous (four Cs and a C-). The source grade is also close, ranging from B to A-. The correspondent grade swings from D to B, which probably reflects how differently he treated each agent rather than inconsistent standards. The overall grades cluster at B- to B, but three writers (B, D and E) grade the overall above the average of their own rows. I trust E's grades least, because its overall comes with no reason at all.

## One note to the writers

Before you hand in the card, check that each overall grade follows from the rows above it, and leave your own pending requests out of a document that is meant to be about him.
